## 1. The Paradox of the “Qualified” Unemployed

My journey in education began in the Democratic Republic of Congo, where I studied pedagogy with the idealistic belief that a degree was a guaranteed ticket to a stable life. Today, finishing my International Relations degree, that idealism has been tempered by a harsh reality. One of the most painful realities for many young Africans today is watching talented graduates leave university full of hope, only to spend years navigating unemployment, unstable work, or endless unpaid opportunities.

We are witnessing a profound paradox. While access to schooling has expanded across the continent, **approximately 72 million young Africans aged 15 to 24 remain untrained or unemployed**. Many of these graduates are “queuing” for a dwindling number of high-status public sector roles, waiting years for a “good” job while the private sector starves for technical talent. 

The bottleneck has shifted: the crisis is no longer *getting into school*; it is **becoming employable**. We are producing graduates, but we are not necessarily producing the builders and fixers the market actually needs.

---

## 2. The Great Mismatch: Why Degrees Aren’t Enough

The “Skills Mismatch” is not a mere perception; it is a measurable economic crisis. African Development Bank data reveals a staggering misalignment across the continent:
- **17.5%** of employed youth are overskilled
- **28.9%** are underskilled
- **8.3%** are overeducated
- A massive **56.9%** are undereducated for their current roles

This desperation for higher education stems from what economists term the **“convexity of returns.”** In Sub-Saharan Africa, primary education offers very low marginal gains. The likelihood of securing wage employment only increases significantly once a student reaches secondary and tertiary levels. This creates a frantic rush toward university, yet our institutions remain disproportionately focused on humanities and theoretical social sciences, while extractive industries, logistics, infrastructure, and agri-business struggle to recruit qualified technical staff.

### The Disconnect in Numbers

- **Egypt:** 1.5 million young people are unemployed, yet private sector firms report over 600,000 vacant positions they cannot fill due to missing technical competencies.
- **South Africa:** There are 3 million young people in the NEET (Not in Education, Employment, or Training) category and 600,000 unemployed university graduates, while 800,000 vacancies remain open across the economy.
- **Democratic Republic of the Congo:** Institutions such as the **INPP** increasingly emphasize professional qualification and technical training because employers continuously struggle to find workers with directly applicable, hands-on competencies.

## 3. The HR Perspective: What “Employability” Actually Means

As an HR-certified professional, I view a degree as only one single variable in the **Employability Equation**. 

In the hiring world, we constantly deal with **“asymmetric information”**—the reality that an employer cannot deduce a candidate’s true capabilities simply by looking at a diploma. A degree proves you can pass exams; it does not prove you can solve novel problems, communicate effectively across multidisciplinary teams, or adapt to high-velocity work environments.

To mitigate this information gap, we must transition toward **dual training systems** that bridge classroom theory with industry reality. As noted in recent labor market research:

> *“Programmes that combine on-the-job and in-class training… can have a significant positive impact on employment and earnings.”*

Modern employability is defined by technical competency combined with essential professional soft skills—such as rigorous time management, active listening, structured problem-solving, and continuous learning. We must dismantle the pervasive culture of *personal connections* and *favoritism*, replacing it with certified, demonstrated skills that solve the information gap for employers.

---

## 4. TVET and the Rise of Skills-Based Employment

For decades, Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) was treated in many African societies as a secondary path reserved for students who failed to enter university. Yet this mindset ignored an indispensable truth: **economies do not function on degrees alone. They function on practical competence.**

In the Democratic Republic of Congo, institutions like the **Institut National de Préparation Professionnelle (INPP)** illustrate why technical training is becoming central to the employability debate. Founded after independence to address severe shortages of qualified workers, the INPP was designed to strengthen professional qualifications and prepare Congolese workers for real labor market needs. Today, it provides specialized training in:
- Industrial mechanics & hydraulics
- Electrical installation & industrial automation
- Construction & civil works
- Welding & precision machining
- Digital professional certifications linked directly to private sector employment

What makes this shift so consequential is that modern African labor markets increasingly reward applied skills over academic prestige alone. Across high-growth sectors such as mining, renewable energy, logistics, cold-chain maintenance, and digital services, employers are searching for professionals who can immediately deliver in real operational environments.

Furthermore, the nature of technical work itself has evolved. A modern technician today frequently operates digital diagnostic tools, mobile IoT telemetry, cloud-based inventory workflows, and software-assisted equipment. The future of employability in Africa will not belong exclusively to office workers with theoretical knowledge, but to skilled professionals capable of fixing, building, and maintaining modern infrastructure.

Institutions like the INPP challenge the outdated assumption that dignity and upward mobility only stem from traditional universities. In reality, Africa’s economic transformation requires electricians, mechanics, renewable energy technicians, software developers, and machinists just as much as lawyers, diplomats, and corporate administrators.

## 5. The Informal Reality and Working Poverty

We must also confront an inescapable economic fact: **90% of jobs in Africa are informal.** This informality is particularly severe in the agricultural sector, where rates are 4.8 times higher than the rest of the world. 

For the majority of young Africans, finding *work* is not the primary dilemma—it is finding work that **pays a living wage**. We face an acute crisis of **Working Poverty**, where 34% of employed African youth still live below the international poverty line of \$2.15 a day.

> *“Many young people are working, but their work does not always translate into higher living standards… more work often coincides with higher poverty.”*

This hidden struggle reveals that millions of young people are forced to leave education prematurely for low-productivity subsistence roles that provide zero upward mobility. Genuine employability must elevate living standards, not merely offer a means of surviving until tomorrow.

---

## 6. Navigating the Future: Practical Reflections for the African Graduate

As a fellow graduate, I offer no hollow “dream big” clichés. Navigating today’s landscape demands strategic pragmatism.

### The Pragmatic Graduate’s Checklist

1. **Prioritize Dual Training:** Seek out apprenticeships, internships, and project-based roles immediately. They cultivate the soft skills—professionalism, stakeholder management, and resilience—that lecture halls cannot simulate.
2. **Bridge the Information Gap:** If your degree is in a broad or theoretical field, supplement it with specialized, verifiable certifications (in Human Resources, Data Analysis, Cloud Tools, or Technical Trades). These serve as credible signals to recruiters, drastically reducing hiring hesitation.
3. **Leverage Digital Penetration:** Data proves that mobile infrastructure and ICT access significantly decrease youth unemployment across Sub-Saharan Africa. Use digital platforms not just as passive consumers, but as launchpads for service delivery, remote consulting, and specialized skill acquisition.
4. **De-romanticize Premature Entrepreneurship:** Starting a venture is a noble endeavor, but launching without working capital, technical mentorship, or market validation often becomes another form of precarious informal employment. Build deep technical competence and industry exposure before you build the firm.

---

## 7. Conclusion: The New Purpose of Education

We stand at a critical crossroads, transitioning from **Education for Status** to **Education for Competence**. My pedagogical foundation in the DRC and my international relations studies have reinforced a fundamental lesson: *a degree is a bridge, not a destination.*

If that bridge does not lead to the real economy, it is failing our generation. Africa does not need more empty credentials; it needs builders, fixers, and innovators who generate tangible value for their societies.

The ultimate question for every student and professional is no longer: *“What is my degree?”*  
It is: **“What can I build, fix, or solve for my community once I have it?”**
